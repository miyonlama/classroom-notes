Counting and Discrete Probability — Important Questions

Questions from the nine past papers (2078–2082). Frequency and marks are transcribed from Past Questions.pdf. Answers will be added after review.

Explain the product rule. How many strings of four lowercase letters contain the letter x at least once?

Frequency: 2078 (Q10) · Marks: 5

[5 marks] Product rule. If a task is performed as a sequence of k independent stages, with n₁ ways to do stage 1, n₂ ways to do stage 2 after that, and so on, then the total number of outcomes is n₁n₂⋯nₖ. The rule follows because each choice at one stage can be paired with every choice at the next: a tree diagram of the process has n₁ branches at level 1, each splitting into n₂, giving n₁n₂ leaves, and so on.

Applying it. A string of four lowercase letters has 26 choices for each position, giving 26⁴ = 456,976 total strings. This is the sample space of all four-letter words over the alphabet.

Complementary counting. A string contains the letter x at least once precisely when it is not a string made entirely from the other 25 letters. There are 25⁴ = 390,625 such strings, so the required count is 26⁴ − 25⁴ = 456,976 − 390,625 = 66,351.

Check by inclusion–exclusion. Let Aᵢ be the set of strings whose i-th letter is x. Then |Aᵢ| = 26³, |Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ| = 26², |Aᵢ ∩ Aⱼ ∩ Aₖ| = 26, and |A₁ ∩ A₂ ∩ A₃ ∩ A₄| = 1. Inclusion–exclusion gives 4·26³ − 6·26² + 4·26 − 1 = 70,304 − 4,056 + 104 − 1 = 66,351, confirming the result.

Why complement is easier. Direct counting requires four separate cases (exactly one, two, three or four x’s); the complement needs one subtraction. When a problem says “at least one”, consider counting the absence of the property instead. This pattern recurs throughout discrete probability.

Why do we need the principle of inclusion–exclusion? Then answer the two parts below.
  1. How many 4-character strings are there that end with a digit and whose first three characters are lowercase alphabets, with no character repeated?
  2. Using induction, show that 3ⁿ − 1 is a multiple of 2 for n ≥ 1.

Frequency: 2079 (Q2) · Marks: 2+3+5

[10 marks] Part (a) — 2 marks: inclusion–exclusion. For finite sets, |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. Adding the two sizes counts elements of the overlap twice, so it must be subtracted once. For three sets, |A ∪ B ∪ C| = |A| + |B| + |C| − |A∩B| − |A∩C| − |B∩C| + |A∩B∩C|. In general, add sizes of single sets, subtract pairwise intersections, add triple intersections, and alternate signs. The principle is needed whenever properties overlap and a naive sum would double count.

Part (b) — 3 marks: the 4-character string. The first three characters are lowercase letters with no repetition: 26 choices for the first, 25 for the second, 24 for the third. The last character is a digit: 10 choices. All four stages are independent, so the product rule gives 26 × 25 × 24 × 10 = 15,600 × 10 = 156,000.

Part (c) — 5 marks: induction for 3ⁿ − 1. Claim: 3ⁿ − 1 is a multiple of 2 for every integer n ≥ 1, that is, 3ⁿ − 1 = 2m for some integer m.

Basis step. For n = 1, 3¹ − 1 = 2 = 2·1, which is a multiple of 2.

Inductive hypothesis. Assume 3ᵏ − 1 = 2m for an arbitrary k ≥ 1, so 3ᵏ = 2m + 1.

Inductive step. 3ᵏ⁺¹ − 1 = 3·3ᵏ − 1 = 3(2m + 1) − 1 = 6m + 2 = 2(3m + 1). Since 3m + 1 is an integer, 3ᵏ⁺¹ − 1 is a multiple of 2, which proves P(k+1).

Conclusion. By the principle of mathematical induction the claim holds for all n ≥ 1. A shorter modular proof is 3 ≡ 1 (mod 2), hence 3ⁿ ≡ 1ⁿ ≡ 1 (mod 2) and 3ⁿ − 1 ≡ 0 (mod 2); the induction proof spells out the same idea step by step.

Verification of the count. To check 26·25·24 = 15,600: 26·25 = 650 and 650·24 = 15,600. Multiplying by 10 gives 156,000. No digit restriction interacts with the letters, so no inclusion–exclusion correction is needed in part (b).

Why inclusion–exclusion is needed in general. When counting strings that avoid several forbidden patterns, the sets of strings containing each pattern overlap. Summing their sizes counts a string with two forbidden features twice. The alternating formula corrects this: add single sets, subtract pairwise overlaps, add triple overlaps, and so on until no new intersection remains. In part (b) the no-repetition rule is enforced directly by reducing the number of choices at each stage, so no correction term is necessary, but in the “at least one x” problem the complement was simpler than tracking overlaps.

Proof of the two-set formula. Split A ∪ B into three disjoint pieces: A \ B, B \ A and A ∩ B. Then |A| = |A \ B| + |A ∩ B| and |B| = |B \ A| + |A ∩ B|. Adding gives |A| + |B| = |A ∪ B| + |A ∩ B|, because the union is the disjoint sum of the three pieces. Rearranging yields |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. The general formula for many sets follows by induction on the number of sets.

What is the generalized pigeonhole principle? If a class has 24 students, what is the minimum number of possible distinct grades required to ensure that there are at least two students with the same grade?

Frequency: 2079 (Q11) · Marks: 5

[5 marks] Principle. If N objects are placed into k boxes, then some box contains at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects. Otherwise each box would hold at most ⌈N/k⌉ − 1 objects and the total would be at most k(⌈N/k⌉ − 1) < N, a contradiction. The ordinary pigeonhole principle is the special case N > k, where some box holds at least two.

Setting up the grades problem. Treat the 24 students as objects and the possible grades as boxes. We want the smallest number k of distinct grades that still forces two students to share a grade. By the principle, a shared grade is forced when 24 > k.

Applying it. With k = 24 grades, 24 students could each receive a different grade, so no repetition is forced. With k = 23, we have 24 > 23, so some grade is assigned to at least ⌈24/23⌉ = 2 students. The minimum number of possible distinct grades that guarantees a shared grade is therefore 23.

Checking the boundary. If only 22 grades existed, the guarantee would still hold, since ⌈24/22⌉ = 2; but 23 is the largest k that works, and the question asks for the minimum number of distinct grades required to ensure the sharing, which is 23. Had the question asked for the largest k allowing all distinct grades, the answer would be 24.

Typical uses. The principle proves that among 13 people two share a birth month, that among N+1 integers two have the same remainder modulo N, and that any sequence of n² + 1 distinct numbers has a monotone subsequence of length n + 1. It is a counting argument that guarantees existence without constructing the pair.

What does connectivity in graphs mean? Differentiate between permutation and combination. Solve the recurrence relation aₙ = 7aₙ₋₁ − 10aₙ₋₂ with initial conditions a₀ = 2 and a₁ = 3.

Frequency: 2080.1 (Q3) · Marks: 4+6

[10 marks] Part (a) — 4 marks: connectivity. A graph is connected when for every pair of vertices there is a path joining them; otherwise its maximal connected pieces are its connected components. Connectivity is a structural property about reachability and says nothing about counting selections.

Permutation versus combination. A permutation is an ordered arrangement of objects; a combination is an unordered selection. Choosing three of five objects gives C(5,3) = 10 combinations but P(5,3) = 60 ordered triples, since each chosen triple can be arranged in 3! = 6 ways. Use a permutation when positions or roles differ, a combination when only membership matters.

Part (b) — 6 marks: the recurrence. Solve aₙ = 7aₙ₋₁ − 10aₙ₋₂ with a₀ = 2 and a₁ = 3. The characteristic equation is r² − 7r + 10 = (r − 2)(r − 5) = 0, with distinct roots 2 and 5. Hence the general solution is aₙ = A·2ⁿ + B·5ⁿ.

Finding the constants. Substituting n = 0 gives A + B = 2. Substituting n = 1 gives 2A + 5B = 3. Doubling the first equation gives 2A + 2B = 4; subtracting from the second gives 3B = −1, so B = −1/3 and A = 2 + 1/3 = 7/3. Therefore aₙ = (7·2ⁿ − 5ⁿ)/3.

Checking initial terms. For n = 0 the formula gives (7 − 1)/3 = 2 = a₀. For n = 1 it gives (14 − 5)/3 = 3 = a₁. For n = 2 the recurrence gives 7·3 − 10·2 = 1, and the formula gives (28 − 25)/3 = 1, so the closed form agrees.

Why the characteristic method works. Trying a solution of the form rⁿ and dividing by rⁿ⁻² turns the recurrence into the characteristic polynomial. Linearity then lets us combine the two independent solutions; two initial conditions determine the two constants uniquely. Repeated or complex roots require modified forms such as (A + Bn)rⁿ.

Connection to the other parts. Recurrences count arrangements with repeated structure, just as permutations and combinations count arrangements and selections. Connectivity, by contrast, is a graph property; keeping the three ideas separate avoids mixing different kinds of counting.

Worked permutation and combination numbers. If the graph question asked for a spanning tree chosen from the complete graph on 5 vertices, Cayley’s formula gives 5³ = 125 trees. If instead we count ordered selections of 3 vertices from 5, P(5,3) = 5·4·3 = 60; the unordered selections number C(5,3) = 10. The factor 6 between them is 3!, the number of orderings of the three chosen objects.

Checking connectivity separately. A graph on n vertices is connected when a path exists between every pair. Testing this by breadth-first search from one vertex takes O(|V| + |E|) time and succeeds exactly when the search reaches all n vertices. This is unrelated to the closed form above, which solves a counting recurrence; the two parts of the question deliberately test different techniques.

State the pigeonhole principle. Prove that C(n + 1, k) = C(n, k − 1) + C(n, k) for positive integers n and k with n ≥ k.

Frequency: 2080.1 (Q9) · Marks: 5

[5 marks] Pigeonhole principle. If N objects are placed into k boxes and N > k, then at least one box contains two or more objects. More generally some box contains at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects. The proof is by contradiction: if every box held at most one, the total would be at most k, contradicting N > k.

Claim. C(n + 1, k) = C(n, k − 1) + C(n, k) for positive integers n and k with n ≥ k. This is Pascal’s identity, the rule that builds Pascal’s triangle.

Combinatorial proof. Count the k-element subsets of a set S with n + 1 elements by distinguishing one element x. Subsets that exclude x are k-element subsets of the remaining n elements: C(n, k) choices. Subsets that include x are formed by choosing k − 1 further elements from those n elements: C(n, k − 1) choices. The two cases are disjoint and exhaust all k-subsets, so adding them gives C(n + 1, k).

Algebraic proof. C(n, k − 1) + C(n, k) = n!/(k−1)!(n−k+1)! + n!/k!(n−k)! = n![k + (n − k + 1)]/[k!(n − k + 1)!] = n!(n + 1)/[k!(n + 1 − k)!] = (n + 1)!/[k!(n + 1 − k)!] = C(n + 1, k).

Numeric check. Take n = 4, k = 2: C(5,2) = 10 and C(4,1) + C(4,2) = 4 + 6 = 10. The identity is the reason each entry of Pascal’s triangle is the sum of the two entries above it, and it underpins the binomial theorem’s recursive structure.

Boundary cases. For k = 0 the identity reads C(n+1,0) = C(n,−1) + C(n,0), where C(n,−1) = 0 by convention and both sides equal 1. For k = n+1 it gives C(n+1,n+1) = C(n,n) + C(n,n+1) = 1 + 0. These edge cases confirm that the combinatorial interpretation remains consistent at the ends of each row.

What is a permutation? What is the next permutation in lexicographic order after 362541?

Frequency: 2080.1 (Q10) · Marks: 5

[5 marks] Permutation. A permutation of a set is an ordered arrangement of its elements. The number of permutations of n distinct objects is n!, since there are n choices for the first position, n − 1 for the second, and so on. Lexicographic order lists permutations as dictionary words, comparing them symbol by symbol from the left.

Algorithm for the next permutation. (1) Scan from right to left to find the longest suffix that is nonincreasing. (2) If the whole string is nonincreasing, it is the last permutation and there is no next one. (3) Let the pivot be the digit just before that suffix. (4) In the suffix, find the smallest digit greater than the pivot. (5) Swap that digit with the pivot. (6) Reverse the suffix to make it increasing, which gives the smallest possible tail.

Applying it to 362541. Scanning from the right: 4 > 1, 5 > 4, but 2 < 5, so the pivot is 2 and the suffix is 5, 4, 1, already decreasing. The smallest digit in the suffix greater than 2 is 4. Swapping gives 3, 6, 4, 5, 2, 1, and reversing the tail 5, 2, 1 gives 1, 2, 5.

Result. The next permutation is 364125. It is the least arrangement strictly greater than 362541: any arrangement starting 362 must have a tail at most 541, and among those starting 364 the smallest tail from the remaining digits {1, 2, 5} is 125.

Why reversing works. After the swap the suffix is still decreasing because the pivot was replaced by the smallest larger digit. Reversing a decreasing suffix makes it increasing, which is the lexicographically smallest ordering of those digits. The algorithm therefore produces the immediate successor in O(n) time.

State the generalized pigeonhole principle. How many three-digit integers can be formed without repeating any digit?

Frequency: 2080.2 (Q6) · Marks: 5

[5 marks] Generalized pigeonhole principle. When N objects occupy k boxes, some box holds at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects. It guarantees repetition when N > k, but it is not the rule used to count arrangements with distinct digits; that count uses the product rule with sampling without replacement.

Counting the digits. A three-digit integer has a hundreds digit from 1 to 9, since a leading zero would make it a two-digit number: 9 choices. The tens digit may be any of 0 to 9 except the digit already used: 10 − 1 = 9 choices. The units digit must differ from both previous digits: 10 − 2 = 8 choices.

Product rule. The three choices are independent in sequence, so the count is 9 × 9 × 8 = 648.

Checking the total. There are 900 three-digit integers from 100 to 999. Of these, those with a repeated digit number 900 − 648 = 252. A direct count of the complement agrees: patterns with all three digits equal contribute 9, and patterns with exactly two equal contribute 243, giving 252.

Contrast with repetition allowed. If digits could repeat, the count would be 9 × 10 × 10 = 900. The reduction to 648 shows the effect of the no-repetition condition, which is a sampling-without-replacement constraint rather than a pigeonhole phenomenon. Equivalently, the count is P(10,3) − P(9,2), the permutations of three digits from ten minus those beginning with zero: 720 − 72 = 648.

State the generalized pigeonhole principle. How many four-digit integers can be formed without repeating any digit?

Frequency: 2081 (Q7) · Marks: 5

[5 marks] Generalized pigeonhole principle. If N objects are distributed among k boxes, some box contains at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects. With four positions and ten digit symbols, a pigeonhole argument would say that among eleven chosen digits some value repeats; here we instead impose that no digit repeats, so we count without replacement.

Counting positions. The thousands digit cannot be 0, giving 9 choices (1 through 9). The hundreds digit may be 0 but must differ from the thousands digit: 9 choices. The tens digit must differ from the two used digits: 8 choices. The units digit must differ from the three used digits: 7 choices.

Product rule. Multiplying the successive counts gives 9 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 81 × 56 = 4536 four-digit integers with no repeated digit.

Comparison with the three-digit case. The three-digit count was 9 × 9 × 8 = 648; adding a fourth position multiplies by 7, giving 648 × 7 = 4536. Each additional position has one fewer available digit because of the no-repetition condition.

Sanity check. The largest four-digit number with distinct digits is 9876 and the smallest is 1023. The count 4536 is a plausible fraction of the 9000 four-digit integers; allowing repetition gives 9 × 10³ = 9000, so 4464 numbers are excluded by the constraint. These are counted by inclusion–exclusion over the positions where a digit repeats.

List some advantages of randomized algorithms. Given any set of ten natural numbers between 1 and 99 (inclusive), prove that there exist two disjoint nonempty subsets of the set with equal sums. How do you generalize permutations and combinations?

Frequency: 2082 (Q3) · Marks: 2+4+4

[10 marks] Part (a) — 2 marks: advantages. A randomized algorithm makes random choices during execution. Advantages include simplicity of design, efficiency on large inputs, resistance to adversarial worst-case inputs, and symmetry breaking. Randomized quicksort, for example, is unlikely to encounter its worst case regardless of the input order, and hashing uses randomness to distribute keys evenly.

Part (b) — 4 marks: equal-sum subsets. A set S of ten numbers has 2¹⁰ = 1024 subsets, of which 1023 are nonempty. Each subset sum is a positive integer between 1 and 10 × 99 = 990. Since 1023 > 990, the pigeonhole principle gives two distinct subsets A and B with the same sum.

Removing the overlap. If A and B overlap, remove the intersection from both: let A′ = A \ B and B′ = B \ A. Since the same quantity was removed from each, sum(A′) = sum(B′). The two sets are now disjoint. Neither is empty: if A′ were empty then A ⊂ B, and equality of sums would force B \ A to have sum zero, impossible with positive integers. Hence two disjoint nonempty subsets have equal sums.

Part (c) — 4 marks: generalising permutations and combinations. A permutation counts ordered arrangements; a combination counts unordered selections. Several generalisations extend these ideas. Permutations of a multiset with repeated elements number n!/(n₁!⋯nₖ!), dividing by the factorials of the repetition counts. Circular arrangements of n distinct objects number (n − 1)!, because rotations of one arrangement are identified. Combinations with repetition, choosing k items from n types when repeats are allowed, number C(n + k − 1, k), obtainable by the stars-and-bars method.

Illustrating stars and bars. Choosing 3 items from 4 types with repetition allowed corresponds to arranging 3 stars and 3 bars, giving C(4 + 3 − 1, 3) = C(6,3) = 20 possibilities. The ordinary combination C(4,3) = 4 is the special case with no repetition.

Unifying theme. All these formulas come from the same product and sum rules combined with care about whether order matters, whether objects are distinct, and whether repetition is allowed. Recognising those three questions determines which formula applies.

Why the argument is nonconstructive. The pigeonhole proof guarantees that equal-sum subsets exist but does not identify them. That is typical of counting existence proofs: the conclusion follows from the size of the sample space without producing a witness.

Checking the subset arithmetic. With 1023 nonempty subsets and sums ranging from 1 to 990, the surplus of 33 subsets over the number of possible sums is more than enough to force a collision. Even if every sum value were achieved once, 990 of the subsets would be used and 33 would remain, each duplicating an existing sum. The collision may involve overlapping subsets, which is why the removal step is essential.
