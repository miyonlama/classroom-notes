Counting and Discrete Probability — Important Questions

Questions from the nine past papers (2078–2082). Frequency and marks are transcribed from Past Questions.pdf. Answers will be added after review.

How to read a card

  • Frequency lists every paper and question number where the question appeared.
  • Marks lists every distinct mark the question carried. A question with parts can have more than one value.
  • A repeated question appears once, with all its occurrences in the Frequency line.

Explain the product rule. How many strings of four lowercase letters contain the letter x at least once?

Frequency: 2078 (Q10) · Marks: 5

Product rule. If a task splits into successive independent stages with n₁, n₂, …, nₖ choices, the total number of outcomes is n₁n₂⋯nₖ.

Count strings of four lowercase letters containing x at least once by complementary counting. All strings: 26⁴ = 456,976. Strings with no x: 25⁴ = 390,625. Hence the required number is 26⁴ − 25⁴ = 66,351.

Complementary counting is easier than summing the cases of exactly one, two, three, or four x’s, which would require four separate binomial calculations.

Why do we need the principle of inclusion–exclusion? Then answer the two parts below.
  1. How many 4-character strings are there that end with a digit and whose first three characters are lowercase alphabets, with no character repeated?
  2. Using induction, show that 3ⁿ − 1 is a multiple of 2 for n ≥ 1.

Frequency: 2079 (Q2) · Marks: 2+3+5

Inclusion–exclusion. For finite sets, |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. Simply adding the two sizes counts the overlap twice, so it must be subtracted once; three or more sets alternate adding and subtracting intersections. This avoids double counting when counting outcomes with “at least one” properties.

Part b. A 4-character string ending in a digit, with first three characters lowercase letters and no repetition: 26 × 25 × 24 for the letters and 10 for the final digit, giving 26 · 25 · 24 · 10 = 156,000.

Part c. Prove 3ⁿ − 1 is even for n ≥ 1. Basis: 3¹ − 1 = 2. Assume 3ᵏ − 1 = 2m. Then 3ᵏ⁺¹ − 1 = 3(2m + 1) − 1 = 6m + 2 = 2(3m + 1), which is even. Hence the statement holds for all n ≥ 1.

What is the generalized pigeonhole principle? If a class has 24 students, what is the minimum number of possible distinct grades required to ensure that there are at least two students with the same grade?

Frequency: 2079 (Q11) · Marks: 5

Generalized pigeonhole principle. If N objects are placed into k boxes, some box contains at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects. Otherwise every box would hold at most ⌈N/k⌉ − 1 and the total would be less than N.

Here the 24 students are objects and the possible grades are boxes. To force two students to share a grade, the number of boxes must satisfy k ≤ 23: if only 23 distinct grades are possible, 24 students into 23 grades forces at least ⌈24/23⌉ = 2 students with the same grade by the pigeonhole principle.

What does connectivity in graphs mean? Differentiate between permutation and combination. Solve the recurrence relation aₙ = 7aₙ₋₁ − 10aₙ₋₂ with initial conditions a₀ = 2 and a₁ = 3.

Frequency: 2080.1 (Q3) · Marks: 4+6

Connectivity. A graph is connected when a path joins every pair of vertices. It is a structural property of the graph, unrelated to the counting question that follows.

Permutation vs combination. A permutation is an ordered arrangement; a combination is an unordered selection. Choosing 3 of 5 objects gives C(5, 3) = 10 combinations but P(5, 3) = 60 ordered triples.

Recurrence. For aₙ = 7aₙ₋₁ − 10aₙ₋₂ the characteristic equation is r² − 7r + 10 = (r − 2)(r − 5) = 0, so aₙ = A·2ⁿ + B·5ⁿ. From a₀ = 2 we get A + B = 2; from a₁ = 3 we get 2A + 5B = 3. Solving gives B = −1/3 and A = 7/3, hence aₙ = (7·2ⁿ − 5ⁿ)/3. Check: a₀ = (7 − 1)/3 = 2 and a₁ = (14 − 5)/3 = 3.

State the pigeonhole principle. Prove that C(n + 1, k) = C(n, k − 1) + C(n, k) for positive integers n and k with n ≥ k.

Frequency: 2080.1 (Q9) · Marks: 5

Pigeonhole principle. If more than k objects occupy k boxes, some box holds at least two objects. Its generalized form says some box holds at least ⌈N/k⌉ objects.

Pascal identity. C(n + 1, k) = C(n, k − 1) + C(n, k). A combinatorial proof: to choose k objects from n + 1, either exclude the last object, giving C(n, k) choices, or include it and choose k − 1 from the remaining n, giving C(n, k − 1). These cases are disjoint and exhaustive, so adding them proves the identity.

Algebraically, C(n, k − 1) + C(n, k) = n!/(k−1)!(n−k+1)! + n!/k!(n−k)! = n![k + (n − k + 1)]/k!(n − k + 1)! = (n + 1)!/k!(n + 1 − k)! = C(n + 1, k).

What is a permutation? What is the next permutation in lexicographic order after 362541?

Frequency: 2080.1 (Q10) · Marks: 5

A permutation is an ordered arrangement of distinct objects. Lexicographic order lists permutations as dictionary words.

To get the next permutation of 362541: scan from the right to find the first digit smaller than its right neighbour. Here 2 < 5, so the pivot is 2 and the suffix is 5, 4, 1, which is decreasing (already largest). The smallest suffix digit greater than 2 is 4. Swap 2 and 4 to get 364521, then reverse the suffix 5, 2, 1 into 1, 2, 5.

The next permutation is therefore 364125. This is the least arrangement strictly greater than 362541, because no arrangement beginning 362 can exceed 362541 and 364125 has the smallest possible tail after 364.

State the generalized pigeonhole principle. How many three-digit integers can be formed without repeating any digit?

Frequency: 2080.2 (Q6) · Marks: 5

A three-digit integer has a nonzero hundreds digit, so there are 9 choices (1–9) for the first digit. The tens digit may be 0 but must differ from the first: 9 choices. The units digit must differ from the first two: 8 choices.

By the product rule the number of three-digit integers with no repeated digit is 9 × 9 × 8 = 648.

The generalized pigeonhole principle is the underlying counting idea when digits are treated as boxes: once a digit is used it cannot be reused, so each successive position has one fewer available digit.

State the generalized pigeonhole principle. How many four-digit integers can be formed without repeating any digit?

Frequency: 2081 (Q7) · Marks: 5

A four-digit integer has 9 choices for the first digit (1–9), then 9 for the second (0–9 except the first), 8 for the third, and 7 for the fourth.

By the product rule the count is 9 × 9 × 8 × 7 = 4536 four-digit integers with no repeated digit.

Contrast this with repetitions allowed: 9 × 10³ = 9000. The repeated-digit restriction removes 4464 of those strings.

List some advantages of randomized algorithms. Given any set of ten natural numbers between 1 and 99 (inclusive), prove that there exist two disjoint nonempty subsets of the set with equal sums. How do you generalize permutations and combinations?

Frequency: 2082 (Q3) · Marks: 2+4+4

Randomized algorithms use random choices in their logic. Advantages include simplicity, resistance to adversarial worst-case inputs, speed on large structured problems, and the ability to break symmetry, as in randomized quicksort or hashing.

Equal-sum subsets. A set of 10 numbers has 2¹⁰ − 1 = 1023 nonempty subsets. Each subset sum lies between 1 and 10 × 99 = 990. Since 1023 > 990, the pigeonhole principle gives two distinct subsets A and B with equal sums. If they intersect, remove A ∩ B from each: the remainders A \ B and B \ A are still equal in sum and are disjoint. Neither is empty, because equal sums with positive integers rule out one being a proper subset of the other. Hence two disjoint nonempty subsets have equal sums.

Generalizing permutations and combinations. Permutations of a multiset divide by repeated factorials, n!/(n₁!⋯nₖ!); circular permutations of n objects are (n − 1)!; combinations with repetition of k objects from n types number C(n + k − 1, k). These extend the basic ordered/unordered counts to repeated or structured selections.
